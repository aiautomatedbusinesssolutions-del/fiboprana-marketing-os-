For thirty years, one idea about your mind was treated as a joke by most of science.
This week, a neuroscientist asked everyone to look again.

The idea comes from Roger Penrose, a Nobel laureate in physics. In the nineteen nineties
he proposed that human thought does things no computer can do, and so the mind must be
using quantum mechanics, the strange physics of the very small, in a way that cannot be
copied by any machine. He never denied that neurons compute. His claim was narrower and
stranger. That conscious understanding cannot be reduced to computation alone.

Most scientists found that laughable. The objection was simple. Quantum effects need
coordination between the parts of a system, and that coordination is fragile. It usually
survives only at temperatures far below zero, because at anything warmer, random molecular
movement shakes it apart. The brain is warm and wet. Case closed.

Then, on September first, a professor of neuroscience at Wellesley College named Mike
Wiest published an argument that the case was never actually closed. He says recent
experiments have found stable quantum states at far higher temperatures than anyone
thought possible, and that the brain-is-too-warm objection was never conclusive. He is
careful to say the idea is still fringe, and that most of his colleagues still disagree
with him. But he is asking for a second look.

He is not the only one lately saying the mind is stranger than a switch. In August,
three scientists at MIT published a review in the Journal of Neuroscience arguing that
the brain does not compute like a digital circuit, one step at a time. They say it
computes with traveling waves of rhythmic activity, in analog, many calculations at once.
In their words, circuits and synapses are the start, but there is more going on. The
brain exploits its own physics.

So here is where the science actually stands. Two serious camps, one quantum and one
wave, published within weeks of each other. They do not agree with each other. Nobody
has settled it. The only thing they share is a refusal to believe the mind is a machine
made of on and off switches.

Now go back two thousand years.

The Dhammapada, a collection of the Buddha's sayings, opens with the mind. Mind precedes
all mental states. Mind is their chief. They are all mind-wrought. The Yoga Sutras of
Patanjali, the classical manual of meditation, opens with a method and a claim. Restrain
the thought-streams natural to the mind, and then the seer dwells in his own nature.
Otherwise, he is of the same form as the thought-streams.

Read that slowly. There is a stream of thoughts, and there is something that watches it,
and the way to find out is not to theorize. It is to sit and still the stream, then look at what remains.
The same text adds that this meditation only becomes well-grounded when it is continued,
without interruption, over a long period of time.

The traditions did not predict quantum physics. Do not let anyone tell you they did. They
made a first-person claim about the mind and the one observing it, and the lab makes a
physics claim about what the mind is made of. What they share is this. Neither one
believes you are a machine. And where the lab built theories, the traditions built a
method, one you can do tonight, no equipment required.

That is the part I keep coming back to. The mind may be stranger and more capable than
most of us were taught. The mechanisms are still being argued about, and that argument may
run for decades. You do not have to wait for it.

Here is one place to start. Notice the moment just before you react. Someone says the
thing, the situation is designed to pull a reaction out of you, and there is a half
second where the pull is visible. Hold your awareness there. Watch the pull instead of
becoming it. Nothing about that requires settling the physics. It only requires noticing.
And the more you notice, the more there is to notice. The stream and the one watching
it were written down two thousand years ago. You can check it yourself tonight.
